## Context

A logistics operator: receiving, warehousing, order picking and distribution. An
operation where mistakes aren't debated in a meeting — you see them the next
morning in a truck that left wrongly loaded.

My role sat across three things that in most organisations live apart: **the
management system, the information systems, and shop-floor improvement**. Keeping
them together is what made any of them work.

## The problem

A certified management system and a real operation tend to drift apart. The
procedure says one thing, the operator does another, and the binder gets updated
the week before the audit. When that happens, certification becomes an
administrative cost that improves nothing.

The challenge wasn't getting certified. It was **certifying something people
would actually use**.

## The three pieces

### 1. The tri-standard management system

Implementation and certification of **ISO 9001, 14001 and 45001** as an
integrated system, plus a full **recertification** cycle — which is the real
test: keeping the system alive once the pressure of the first audit is gone.

Certification was run **as a project**, not as a quality task: scope, schedule,
owners and milestones through to the external audit. That's the difference
between arriving on the date and arriving out of breath.

**Documentation.** Creating and maintaining forms, procedures and work
instructions. The principle that held it together: **document what someone is
going to read**. A work instruction nobody opens isn't a control, it's a
liability — you have to maintain it, audit it and explain it, and it contributes
nothing.

### 2. ERP and CRM

Running a **SAP module** alongside an off-the-shelf system. Two tools with
different logic over the same operation — the usual situation, and the most
uncomfortable one: you have to decide which is the source of truth for each piece
of data, and then hold that decision.

**End-to-end inventory management:** making system stock and warehouse stock say
the same thing. It sounds obvious, and it's where the credibility of everything
else is decided — if inventory lies, no indicator is worth anything.

### 3. Continuous improvement on the floor

**World Class Manufacturing** and **Lean** applied to logistics operations.
Standardisation, waste elimination, visual indicators where the work happens.

And something that rarely gets told: **training**. I built and delivered the
sessions that walked people through the processes. A process that only exists in
a PDF doesn't exist — it exists once the person running it understands **why**
it works that way.

## Stakeholder management

Management, operations, customers and external auditors all want different things
from the same information. Management wants the summary, operations wants
actionable detail, the customer wants compliance, and the auditor wants
traceability.

Sustaining one system that serves all four — without four parallel systems — was
a good part of the job.

## Outcome

- **Certification and recertification** of all three standards passed.
- The system **gets used**: records live where the work happens, not in a binder
  someone fills in before the audit.
- **[Fill in]** what improved in numbers: complaints, inventory shrinkage,
  picking time, incidents.

## What I took from it

That continuous improvement and the management system are the same thing seen
from two sides. Managed separately, the system turns into bureaucracy and the
improvement turns informal — and neither one holds.
